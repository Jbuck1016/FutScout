What a sequence is, and how to read these

A sequence is one unbroken spell of possession by one team. It begins when a side takes control of the ball and ends when they lose it, the game stops, or a set piece intervenes. Coverage varies by competition and season; the current query selects up to twelve candidates. They are the unit this platform reasons in, because a possession is the smallest thing that has a shape: a start, a route, and an outcome.

Why possessions rather than passes. Counting passes tells you volume. A possession tells you intent. Ten passes that go sideways in front of a low block and three that split it are the same on a pass chart and completely different here, because the sequence records where the ball started, where it travelled, and whether it produced anything.

Reading a card

  • Each card is one possession, drawn start to finish, attacking left to right.
  • The hollow ring is where the team won the ball.
  • Arrows are passes; small dots are events with no destination, such as a touch, a take-on or a shot.
  • Dotted segments are movement the feed does not record as an event, usually a carry or a flick. The ball moved, we just cannot see how, so the segment is inferred.
  • A diamond marks the shot. Anything drawn faintly after it is a rebound or second phase, not part of the original move.
  • xT is the threat the possession added by moving the ball into more dangerous areas. No league-average benchmark is supplied by this view; compare the displayed values within their stated source context, not against an invented baseline.

What the type filter means

Possessions are tagged by how they started and how they travelled: from deep, won high, patient build, over the top, switches of play, wide combinations, hold-up play. Picking a type shows the highest-threat examples of that pattern in the current data pool, which is the quickest way to see what a route actually looks like when it works.

Finding similar possessions

Select any card and the tool lists the closest matches in the current data pool under Nearest matches, comparing fourteen standardised features: where it started and ended, how far it travelled vertically and laterally, how many passes, how long they were, the threat generated and how much of it happened in the attacking third. Distance is the measure, so lower is a closer match. It is comparing shape, not teams and not quality, which is why a Miami move can match a Vancouver one. Select a nearest match to overlay it on the picked sequence; the compare tray holds up to three overlays.

One honest limit: sequences describe the team in possession. The opponent’s shape, and any pressure that forced a decision, is invisible in event data. A possession that looks laboured may have been the only option available.
